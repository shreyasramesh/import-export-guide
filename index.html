How to Make Your Own Physical Product

A practical, end-to-end guide to turning an idea into a manufactured product: the real costs, how to find and vet factories, how samples become mass production, how to negotiate without damaging the product, and how to protect cash flow and the product itself.

Why a physical product is different

Software is now cheap to clone, so software alone is rarely a moat. A physical product cannot be cloned in a weekend: a copier needs the molds, specifications, components, and supplier relationships, and those take months and capital to build.

A device plus an app — firmware updates, a subscription, useful data — is harder to copy than either alone. Software builders hold an edge here, because hardware companies are usually weak at software.

North star: cash flow, not unit price, is the key measure of a good production lead. Every decision in this guide should be checked against it.

Part 1 — Key terms and landed cost

  • MOQ (minimum order quantity): hundreds of units for an existing product with your logo (OEM / white label); thousands for a fully custom product. MOQ is not a law — it is the volume where the factory stops losing money.
  • Unit price: tiered by volume, but the spread between small and large batches is only about 10% at best. Do not anchor on the 5,000-unit price when you can afford 500.
  • Tooling / NRE: a simple steel mold costs roughly $3,000–$6,000; a complex one starts in the tens of thousands. A product may need several molds. Tooling is paid upfront, before a single real unit exists.
  • Certification: electronics need FCC (US) or CE (Europe) approval to sell legally. A useful shortcut: build on an already-certified radio module and you may not need to redo full certification. Understand compliance before a production run, because it gates the timeline.
  • Samples: usually tens to a couple of hundred dollars plus shipping. Some factories credit sample costs back on an order; most do not.
  • AQL (acceptable quality limit): define before production what is acceptable, barely acceptable, and unacceptable. Both sides agree to the same standard, it goes in the contract, and it becomes the inspection reference.
  • EXW / FOB / DDP: EXW is the price at the factory door — freight, customs, and duty are all on you. FOB means the factory gets the goods onto the ship. DDP means delivered to your door with duties paid. Always ask which basis a quote uses; the same product looks very different under each.
  • Payment terms: the standard structure is 30% deposit and 70% before shipping. Never pay 100% upfront on a first order. A stronger structure is 30% deposit, 40% after an independent third-party inspection passes, and 30% when the goods are loaded or arrive at port. On a multi-million-dollar order, a 10% shift in terms can free about $100,000 of cash flow.
  • Tariffs: many China-made products now pay an older 7.5% or 25% duty plus a newer 12.5% — roughly 20–37.5% on top of the base duty. The small-package de minimis loophole is gone. Look up the HTS code for the product before trusting any math. Routing goods through Vietnam or Thailand to dodge duty is transshipment and is illegal; a factory genuinely based there is a different story.

The landed-cost example

A factory quotes $8 EXW. The real math runs like this:

Factory price (EXW)$8.00$8.00
Onto the ship (FOB value)+$0.40$8.40
Ocean freight+$0.60$9.00
Duty at 37.5% of the on-ship value+$3.15$12.15

EXW is not your cost of goods. Landed cost is.

Pricing rule: retail price must be 3–5× landed cost to cover ads, returns, and retail margins. The $8 product in this example must sell for roughly $48–60. Do this math in a spreadsheet before committing to a product. Try it live in the calculator below.

Part 2 — Find and vet a good factory

Three routes

  1. Alibaba, direct. Many listings are trading companies (middlemen), not factories. Warning signs: a very wide product range, an office-tower address, and a refusal to walk you around the factory floor on a live call. Trading companies are not always bad — they may accept a small first order that a real factory would refuse.
  2. Sourcing agents. On-the-ground partners in China who visit factories and run due diligence for you. They are valuable when you do not speak Chinese.
  3. Trade shows. The Canton Fair in Guangzhou, held every spring and fall, puts about a thousand factories in one hall. It is one of the most efficient sourcing routes available.

Vetting checklist

  • Ask for past case studies, typical order volumes, and annual revenue. Ask to see financial records — factories often share them.
  • Visit in person. Walk the floor and talk to workers directly.
  • Talk to about 10 factories at once. That builds a price benchmark, and factories know it.
  • Check court records on Qichacha, the Chinese company-records platform. Ask whether the factory has been in legal disputes and why. Most of this information exists only on the Chinese web.
  • Look for a factory that has produced for years or decades, holds a real case-study portfolio, has minimal court records, and offers a fair price against the 10-factory benchmark.

Factories mostly assemble, and margins are thin (about 10–20%; see Part 4). The buyer holds the bargaining power — use it tastefully.

Part 3 — From samples to mass production

  • Start with the design file. Begin with a CAD or design file and have the factory run a DFM (design for manufacturing) review: the factory flags what is expensive or impossible to make. Own the design files yourself — not the designer, and not the factory.
  • Timeline. Samples take a few weeks to a few months depending on complexity. Molds add 4–6 weeks. The first mold shots are called T1 samples and are never right the first time — fix and repeat. Production runs 30–45 days after the deposit lands. Then comes inspection, then a few weeks on a ship plus customs. A few months from start to boxes is normal for something simple; longer for anything custom. Delays are normal — plan around them.
  • Holiday traps. Chinese New Year shuts factories for weeks, and a January order is already late. Golden Week, the first week of October, is another slowdown before the fourth quarter.
  • The golden sample. One approved unit, signed and kept by both sides. Every production unit is checked against it. Without one, good quality is an opinion with no reference.
  • Third-party inspection. Hire an inspection agency such as TÜV or Bureau Veritas. A few hundred dollars per inspection buys a detailed report. Brief the inspector on the AQL so they inspect against your standard.
  • Priority and buffers. Small orders get deprioritized behind bigger clients, and a late material delays everything downstream. Strict contractual timeline penalties are nearly impossible to get a factory to accept — plan buffers instead.

Part 4 — Negotiate with factories: seven rules

Relationships (guanxi) matter, but the transaction of value decides. These seven rules protect both the deal and the product.

  1. Negotiate the setup cost, not the MOQ. A first order roughly breaks even for the factory. Asking for 300 units instead of 1,000 marks a buyer as unserious. Solve the factory's problem instead: offer a setup fee, a slightly higher unit price for the smaller run, the standard color instead of a custom one, a mold the factory already owns, or one product instead of five.
  2. Sell the second order. Call the first order a trial. Show the launch plan and the reorder path. Factories rank customers and research buyers — professional profiles, social presence, press. A credible public track record is a strong signal; a near-empty presence is the opposite.
  3. Do not squeeze the unit price. Materials are most of the cost, and factory margins run about 10–20%. Push the price down 10% and the whole profit disappears: the factory may still say yes, and then the plastic gets cheaper, the screws shorter, and the battery smaller. The product quietly becomes worse. The legitimate version of this lever is sourcing materials yourself — finding a better packaging supplier in a lower-cost province and shipping the materials to the factory can save about 10% of total EXW cost.
  4. Talk to the boss, not the sales rep. Reps are typically 20–30 years old, paid a retainer plus roughly 1% of order volume, with no decision power. They are a communication tax between the buyer and the boss. Use WeChat, the channel factories actually run on, rather than relying on email alone.
  5. Give face. Never embarrass anyone in front of their team. Push back privately, one-to-one. Respect is operational, not decorative, in a long-term partnership.
  6. Be the customer factories want. Leverage comes from being easy to work with, not from threatening to walk away. Pay on time, send clear specifications, and never change the design after the mold is cut. When the production line is full, the easy customers' orders run first — and they get the better prices and payment terms.
  7. Protect yourself. Friendly is not naive. If you paid for the molds, the contract states that you own them and that the factory cannot run your product for anyone else. Before sending a design, sign an NNN agreement (non-use, non-disclosure, non-circumvention) written in Chinese and English and enforceable in China — a US NDA does very little there. Register the trademark in China even if you only manufacture there: China awards trademarks to whoever files first, and squatters watch for new brands. Filing in China within six months of a US filing keeps the US priority date. Ask where materials come from: US Customs can hold goods made with Xinjiang-linked inputs (cotton, aluminum, polysilicon) until the supply chain is proven clean. Collect all quotes, benchmark them, and negotiate tastefully rather than auctioning factories against each other.

Part 5 — Cash-flow and protection plays

Sinosure: pay later

Sinosure is China's state-backed export credit insurer. The factory insures your invoice with Sinosure; if you do not pay, Sinosure covers most of the factory's loss, so the factory can safely let you pay later.

  • Best case: net-120 terms after goods ship with zero down. That takes a strong brand and track record and will not happen on a first or second order.
  • The realistic early win is a smaller deposit plus 60–90 day terms. It changes everything, because product can sell before it has been paid for.
  • The factory applies, not the buyer. Sinosure will check the buyer, so expect to show a couple of years of financial statements.
  • The factory pays the insurance premium — ask whether it is built into the price.
  • Many factories have never heard of Sinosure. A factory that already runs Sinosure terms signals experience with international clients.

Schedule A: act against counterfeiters

Anything good gets copied, and takedown notices are a slow, repetitive game. The stronger play is a US federal court action under a Schedule A case, which lists dozens of seller accounts on one schedule.

  • A temporary restraining order goes to major marketplaces; platforms take the listings down and freeze the sellers' payouts.
  • Most defendants never appear, so cases settle or are won by default, and money is collected from the frozen payouts. Some law firms run these cases on contingency.
  • Registered IP must exist first — a US trademark at minimum, and a design patent if the look can be copied. File before launch.
  • Courts have tightened: some judges now want a bond per defendant, and some dismiss cases that lump unrelated sellers together. Use a firm that does this properly.

Add a China 3PL

Most brands run one warehouse (3PL) in the US. Add a second one next to the factory in China: US orders ship from the US warehouse, and the rest of the world ships straight from China, so worldwide selling starts without stocking every country.

The China warehouse is also a backup — when the US warehouse runs dry, it covers US orders while the next shipment is on the water, so advertising never has to switch off. One caution: packages from China into the US now pay full tariffs, so treat the China 3PL as a backup and international lever, not the main US route.

The playbook: starting a hardware product from zero

  1. Do not start fully custom. Find a product a factory already makes and modify it — your logo, your packaging, your tweak. No molds, lower MOQ.
  2. Pre-sell first. A pre-order page or a crowdfunding campaign proves demand. A few thousand dollars of advertising validates the product before any commitment to inventory. The worst outcome is a warehouse of units that do not sell.
  3. Run the landed-cost math before committing. If the product cannot sell for 3–5× landed cost, change the product.
  4. Get samples from every factory worth considering. Samples are the benchmark for quality and for spotting overcharging.
  5. Travel to China and meet every boss in person. Trust runs both ways — the factory is also deciding whether the buyer is legitimate, and both sides put capital into the partnership.
  6. Trademark the product in the US and China.
  7. Only after demand is validated: pay for custom molds, pursue Sinosure terms, and build the real moat.
  8. Assemble one working file: the factory checklist, the questions to ask each factory, and the landed-cost spreadsheet, kept together and updated on every order.

Glossary

Search the key terms used in this guide.

MOQ

Minimum order quantity. Hundreds of units for an existing product with your logo; thousands for a fully custom product. It marks the volume where the factory stops losing money.

OEM

An existing product made by a factory and sold with your logo and packaging — white label. The low-MOQ, no-mold starting point this guide recommends.

NRE / Tooling

The upfront cost of molds and setup. A simple steel mold runs roughly $3,000–$6,000; a complex one starts in the tens of thousands. Paid before any real unit exists.

FCC

US certification that electronics need in order to sell legally. Building on an already-certified radio module may avoid repeating full certification.

CE

The European counterpart to FCC approval. Electronics need CE marking to sell legally in Europe.

AQL

Acceptable quality limit. The agreed standard that defines acceptable, barely acceptable, and unacceptable quality before production starts. Inspections are judged against it.

EXW

Ex Works. The price at the factory door. Freight, customs, and duty are all the buyer's responsibility on top of this price.

FOB

Free On Board. The factory's price includes getting the goods onto the ship. Freight and duty after that point sit with the buyer.

DDP

Delivered Duty Paid. The price covers delivery to the buyer's door with duties already paid — the simplest basis to compare, when it is offered.

HTS code

The classification code in the Harmonized Tariff Schedule that sets a product's duty rate. Look it up before trusting any landed-cost math.

Transshipment

Routing goods through a third country, such as Vietnam or Thailand, to disguise origin and dodge duty. It is illegal. A factory genuinely based in that country is a different case.

DFM

Design for manufacturing. A factory review of a CAD or design file that flags what is expensive or impossible to make, before tooling money is spent.

T1 sample

The first units shot from a new mold. T1 samples are never right the first time; expect fix-and-repeat rounds.

Golden sample

One approved unit, signed and kept by both buyer and factory. Every production unit is checked against this reference.

Guanxi

Relationships and trust in Chinese business culture. It matters, but the transaction of value still decides how a factory treats an order.

NNN agreement

A non-use, non-disclosure, non-circumvention agreement written in Chinese and English and enforceable in China. Stronger protection there than a US NDA.

Sinosure

China's state-backed export credit insurer. A factory insures an invoice with Sinosure so it can safely offer the buyer later payment terms.

Schedule A case

A US federal court action that lists dozens of counterfeit seller accounts on one schedule, so marketplaces take listings down and freeze seller payouts.

3PL

Third-party logistics: an outside warehouse that stores and ships product. This guide recommends pairing a US 3PL with a second warehouse in China.

Landed cost

The true per-unit cost once factory price, freight, duty, brokerage, and inspection are all included. Retail price should be 3–5× landed cost.

Canton Fair

A major trade fair in Guangzhou, held every spring and fall, with about a thousand factories in one hall. One of the most efficient sourcing routes.

Qichacha

A Chinese company-records platform. Use it during factory vetting to check registration details and court records.

WeChat

The messaging channel factories in China actually run on. Direct contact there reaches decision-makers faster than email alone.
